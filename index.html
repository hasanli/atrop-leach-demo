Salt Leaching Simulator

One-dimensional simulation of salt leaching from saline soil by ponded water, based on the advection–dispersion equation and the analytical solution of Ogata and Banks (1961).

Introduction

Salinity limits crop yields on irrigated land. Saline soils are reclaimed by leaching: water is ponded on the surface, infiltrates and carries dissolved salt down, below the root zone. How much salt is removed depends on the amount and the salinity of the leaching water and on how water and salt move through the soil.

The simulator describes this movement with the one-dimensional advection–dispersion equation (ADE) for a homogeneous soil profile from the surface to 200 cm. It starts from the measured salinity at eight depths and calculates the salinity at every depth and time.

Purpose

  • Calculate the salinity profile before leaching, after each water application and at later times.
  • Compare leaching water of different salinity: fresh water, drainage water and their mixtures.
  • Compare the model with the salinity measured in the field, layer by layer, and report the fit.
  • Estimate the salt stored in the profile and the share removed by leaching.

Applications

  • Leaching norms. The water needed (number of applications × depth per application) to lower the salinity of the root zone.
  • Water treatments. The field experiment with fresh water (0.4 g/L), drainage water (39 g/L) and the mixtures 1:1 (≈ 20 g/L) and 1:3 (≈ 29 g/L).
  • Field data. Salinity measured at 25–200 cm before and after each application, entered on the Field data page and compared with the model.
  • Teaching. The effect of flow velocity, dispersion and porosity on salt transport.

Quick start

  1. Parameters: enter the flow velocity v, the dispersion D and the porosity θ; choose the leaching water (C0); enter the initial salinity Ci at each depth or paste a column from Excel.
  2. Setup: the number of applications and the water per application, the depths for the breakthrough curves, and how the samples were taken (layers or points).
  3. Run: press Run (or Enter in a field); the results open. The Run page shows the status and the inputs of the last run.
  4. Results: salinity profiles, breakthrough curves and tables. Export them to Excel, CSV or a PDF report, and the graphs as PNG or SVG.

To compare with measurements, open Field data, enter the measured salinity of a plot and press Run model.

Model

The equation, its classical solution, the solution used for leaching, and the assumptions behind it.

ADE equation

Conservation of a dissolved, non-reactive salt in one dimension, with depth x positive downward:

∂C∂t =D ∂2C∂x2 −v ∂C∂x

Advection (the term with v) moves salt with the water. Dispersion (the term with D) spreads it, because the water moves at different speeds in different pores.

Ogata–Banks (1961)

Ogata and Banks solved the ADE for a semi-infinite medium that is initially free of solute, with the concentration at the inlet raised to C0 at t = 0:

C(x,0)=0 , C(0,t)=C0 , C(∞,t)=0
CC0= A(x,t)= 12 [ erfc⁡(x−vt2Dt) + exp⁡(vxD) erfc⁡(x+vt2Dt) ]

A is the breakthrough fraction: 0 before the water front arrives at depth x, 1 when the applied water has replaced the soil solution there. The second term is small when vx/D is large; the simulator always keeps it. Check value from the reference guide: x = 100 cm, v = 0.10 cm/h, D = 1.0 cm²/h and t = 1000 h give A = 0.5853.

Analytical solution

For leaching the problem is reversed: the soil is saline at the start, and water of salinity C0 is applied at the surface. The initial salinity Ci(x) comes from the measurements at 25, 50 … 200 cm:

C(x,0)= Ci(x) , C(0,t)=C0

The measured profile is a sum of steps of height Δcj at depths zj, Ci(x) = Σ Δcj H(x − zj). The equation is linear, so the solution is the sum of the response to the applied water and the responses to each step:

C(x,t)= C0A(x,t) +∑j Δcj Q(x,t;zj)
Q(x,t;z)= 12 erfc⁡(z−x+vt2Dt) − 12 exp⁡(vxD) erfc⁡(x+z+vt2Dt)

For a uniform initial salinity this reduces to C = Ci + (C0 − Ci)·A. Unlike a depth-by-depth calculation, the sum carries the salt washed out of one layer into the layers below, so a salt front moves down through the profile. The solution agrees with an independent finite-difference calculation within 0.007 g/L.

The applied water sets the time. With the water flux q = vθ, application n of depth W ends at

tn=nWvθ

The salt stored in the profile from 0 to L = 200 cm is S = 0.1 θ ∫ C dx in t/ha (C in g/L, x in cm).

Assumptions

  • Homogeneous soil: v, D and θ are the same at all depths and do not change with time.
  • Vertical, one-dimensional flow. The applications follow each other without pauses; drainage and evaporation between applications are not modelled.
  • Salt is conservative: no sorption, precipitation, dissolution or uptake by plants.
  • Surface: the soil solution at the surface has the salinity of the applied water, C(0, t) = C0 (first-type boundary condition).
  • Bottom: the soil continues below 200 cm with the salinity of the deepest sample, and salt leaves the profile freely (semi-infinite domain).
  • Samples: a layer sample (0–25 cm …) is compared with the model average over that layer; a point sample with the model value at that depth.

Ogata, A. and Banks, R. B. (1961). A solution of the differential equation of longitudinal dispersion in porous media. U.S. Geological Survey Professional Paper 411-A.
